Multi Protocol Smart Home Embedded Linux Gateway - Leonhart

What it does

Leonhart is a custom embedded Linux board capable of hosting local Zigbee and Thread networks, and interacting with smart devices on those networks through Matter commands. This allows for local control of devices, no proprietary walls, and legacy Zigbee devices to remain useful.

What I did

Designed the schematic and PCB from scratch, including DDR4, RGMII Ethernet, power sequencing, eMMC, USB, and radio interfaces.

Rev A successfully booted Linux but exposed power, Ethernet, and radio pinout issues. I debugged those failures and designed Rev B, which boots Linux directly from eMMC with working Gigabit Ethernet, SSH, and MGM240P communication.

Hardware Design

The board was designed as an 8-layer system around the SoC, with the stackup and placement planned around the high-speed interfaces, power integrity, and BGA breakout. DDR4, eMMC, RGMII Ethernet, USB, and the multiprotocol radio were routed directly on the PCB rather than implemented through external modules.

The power is sourced via USB C, and the TPS65219 PMIC supplies the power rails required by the AM6231 and DDR4 subsystem. A separate switching regulator was also used to offset some of the load off the PMIC. The board contains dedicated interfaced for boot configuration, debug, user control, and radio communication.

Bring-Up & Debugging

Given the scope of the project, I designed Rev A with various failsafes, to allow further testing before a second revision would be needed. Such failsafes include USB A in addition to microSD and eMMC, header pins for UART communication to the AM6231 MCU, DNP resistors for configurable bootstrapping, and test points.

Before powering on Rev A, I tested rail impedances to make sure there were no shorts. Then I connected the board to a current limited bench PSU, where I discovered there was a short in one of the components, which I later found was due to a flipped footprint of the SMPS, causing ground and power pads of it to be switched. I removed the SMPS IC, then supplied power through the USB C, but then also bodged the SMPS rail (3.3V) through header pins. Then using the SoC UART header pins, I saw a stream of C characters, generated by the AM6231 boot ROM while waiting for an XMODEM transfer.

I then transferred the three required U-Boot images over UART using TI’s specified serial boot procedure, allowing the system to progress from the ROM bootloader into U-Boot. Once in U-Boot, I was able to check the operation of eMMC(HS200), basic bit manipulation of DDR4, and USB PHY. MicroSD was not working, so I moved to the backup boot method, being USB. In Ubuntu, I prepared the Linux image, dtb, and root filesystem, then successfully booted Linux. I then transferred the Linux files to the eMMC, to prevent having to rely on the unstable USB controller. Below shows the UART terminal displaying the Linux prompt and details of the image and memory.

During the design, I spent a lot of time making sure the sequencing of the rails was correct for the subsystems. The RTL8211 Ethernet physical layer, had very specific rail sequencing requirements, and unfortunately did not work, I believe because the bodged power setup did not meet those requirements. The USB controller was also unpredictable I believe for the same reason, as in Rev B it worked perfectly.

As Ethernet did not work, the last thing I could test was the radio communication. I found out I had wired the radio incorrectly, but had left header pins for another UART peripheral, which I connected to Sparkfun Things Matter MGM240P to confirm the radio communication. Due to the bodged power setup, I opted to not try to boot on power up, as I did not want to damage any components. I then moved on to fixing the issues discovered in Rev A, and then sending Rev B to fabrication.
